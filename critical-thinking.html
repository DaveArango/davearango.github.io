03 · Critical thinking & media literacy

Before sharing, stop and verify.

We live in an environment where information can travel from one screen to thousands of people in seconds. Critical thinking and media literacy help us move from simply receiving information to evaluating it responsibly.

03
Think. Check. Contextualize.

Good information practices require curiosity, evidence, context and the willingness to question our first impression.

Critical thinking

What does it mean?

Critical thinking is the deliberate process of examining information, assumptions and arguments before accepting a conclusion. It involves asking questions, comparing evidence, identifying possible biases, distinguishing facts from interpretations and reaching conclusions that are supported by evidence.

Media literacy

What does it mean?

Media literacy is the ability to access, understand, evaluate and create messages across different media. It means paying attention to who created a message, why it was created, how it is presented, what evidence it uses and how the platform can influence the way the message is received.

Why it matters today

Information is not automatically reliable.

01

Speed changes the way we consume information.

A post can reach a large audience before anyone checks its origin. This makes verification especially important during emergencies, when people are looking for immediate answers.

02

Images and videos can create a false sense of evidence.

A real photograph or video does not necessarily prove the caption attached to it. Content can be old, taken in another country, edited or generated with artificial intelligence.

03

Our own assumptions can influence what we believe.

People naturally pay attention to information that seems to confirm what they already expect. Critical thinking creates a pause between seeing a claim and accepting it.

Real case · Colombia · August 2026
Case study: misinformation after an earthquake

When a real event is surrounded by false or misleading content.

On August 10, 2026, the Colombian Geological Service reported a magnitude 7.4 earthquake with an epicenter in San José del Palmar, Chocó, at a depth of 103 km. The event generated intense public attention and a large amount of content on social media.

Factchequeado documented several misleading examples that circulated after the earthquake. One video was shared as if it showed a school in Colombia during the earthquake, but reverse-image research showed that the video had actually been recorded in a school in the Philippines during a June 2026 earthquake. Another image was shared as if it showed the Monumento a la Resistencia in Cali after the earthquake; the fact-check found that the image had been generated with artificial intelligence.

This case is useful because the underlying event was real. The problem was not whether an earthquake happened, but whether individual pieces of content actually represented what they claimed to represent.

Applying critical thinking

A simple verification routine.

A surprising image or video should not be treated as proof by itself. A practical approach is to slow down and investigate the claim.

Question the claim

What exactly is being claimed? What information is missing?

Check the source

Who published it? Is the original source available? Is the account trustworthy?

Check the context

When and where was the photo, video or statement created?

Compare evidence

Look for independent and authoritative sources before accepting the conclusion.

What the case teaches

A real event can carry false information.

The earthquake was confirmed by the Colombian Geological Service. However, some online posts used authentic or realistic-looking media with an incorrect location, date or origin.

Critical thinking asks whether the evidence supports the specific claim, while media literacy asks us to understand how the message was produced, presented and distributed.

Key idea: Do not ask only, “Does this look real?” Ask, “What evidence connects this content to the claim?”
My conclusion

Being informed is an active process.

Critical thinking and media literacy are not about distrusting everything. They are about becoming more precise about what we know, what we do not know and what evidence supports a claim. In a digital environment, this is especially important because every person can be both a consumer and a distributor of information.

The earthquake case demonstrates why a few seconds of verification can make a meaningful difference: before reacting, commenting or sharing, we can stop, check the source, investigate the context and compare evidence.
